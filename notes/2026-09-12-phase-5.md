# Phase 5 — Living with it

*2026-09-12.* The phase that decides whether this gets used or turned off.

Everything below was decided against the journal and transcript this machine
actually produced over the preceding day, using the replay harness. Nothing here
was tuned from memory or from an argument about what would probably feel right.

---

## The headline

Replayed against 7.8 hours of real desktop history, the ambient narration as
designed would have spoken **14.7 times an hour**, and **fewer than half** of
those utterances would have had any reason behind them beyond a coin flip.

After this phase: **1.2 times an hour, and every one of them carries a signal.**

```
                       before        after
utterances/hour         14.7          1.2
carried a reason        46%           100%
rate vs 15% target      9.6%          16.7%
```

That is the whole phase in three numbers. The rest of this document is how each
one moved, and what had to be wrong for it to be where it was.

> **On "utterances/hour".** It is the least stable of the three, because it
> divides by wall-clock time, and a journal containing hours of idle looks
> quieter than one that doesn't. Re-run afterwards over a 12.1-hour window that
> happened to include two ten-minute idle measurements, the same configuration
> gives **0.8/hour**. Both are correct — the figure describes a *day*, not a
> policy.
>
> The two that do describe the policy, and that held identically across both
> windows, are **~16 % achieved against a 15 % target** and **100 % of
> utterances carrying a reason**. Those are the ones to watch when re-running
> `replay-scoring.py` against a longer journal later.

## 5.1 — Interest scoring, tuned against real data

### Three of the five signals were dead

The attention model in [docs/08](../docs/08-personality-and-config.md) proposed
novelty, rarity, odd-hour, return-after-absence and sequence-oddity. Measured
over 247 scorable journal events:

| signal | fired | |
|---|---|---|
| `novel-today` | 5/247 (2.0%) | effectively inert |
| `rare` | 9/247 (3.6%) | effectively inert |
| `odd-hour` | 30/247 (12.1%) | a blanket time-of-day multiplier |
| `returned` | **0/247 (0.0%)** | **never fired at all** |
| `rapid-switch` | 44/247 (17.8%) | a *penalty* — the only one doing work |

`returned` used a 3600-second absence threshold. The longest gap between any two
desktop events in the whole journal is **3454 seconds**. The threshold sat just
past the end of the data, so the signal docs/08 calls the "natural re-entry
point" had never once been true.

Novelty and rarity failed for a structural reason rather than a tuning one: they
are per-app signals, and this is a three-application desktop — the terminal
(131 events), Claude Code (68), Brave (48). After the first hour of any day
nothing is novel and nothing is rare, permanently.

The consequence is visible in the replay: of the 23 utterances the old scoring
would have produced, **12 carried no interest signal whatsoever**. It was not
selecting for interest. It was a 15% random sampler with a mild late-night bias.

### What replaced them

What *does* vary in the real journal is not which app but the shape of the
session:

```
dwell between focus changes   p25 5.3s | p50 17.3s | p75 89.1s | p90 234.5s | max 3454s
```

That spread is discriminating power, and it is knowable at the moment an event
arrives with no lookahead. So:

| change | rationale |
|---|---|
| `dwell_boost` (new), `dwell_secs: 300` | fires 17/247 (8.1%) — a focus change after five minutes in one window is a real transition |
| `return_secs: 3600 → 600` | fires 8/247 (3.2%) instead of never |
| `return_boost` (new weight) | it was silently multiplying by `novelty_boost`, coupling two dials docs/08 promises are independent |

Novelty, rarity and odd-hour are kept. They fire rarely, but when they fire they
are correct, and they cost nothing. They are simply no longer load-bearing.

### Workspace switches were the loudest thing in the system

docs/01 put workspace switches in the `quiet` tier — the always-on floor,
spoken unconditionally at every verbosity. That was written on the assumption
they are rare and deliberate.

They are the single most frequent thing that happens:

```
90 workspace switches in 7.8 hours
median gap between them: 1.4 seconds
54% arrive within 2 seconds of the one before
largest burst: 9 switches in 9.5 seconds  (1→1→2→3→4→3→4→5→4)
```

Spoken unconditionally, that burst is *"One. One. Two. Three. Four. Three. Four.
Five. Four."* Nine destinations is one journey.

Two fixes, both by analogy with what the focus path already did:

- **`hypr.workspace_settle_secs: 2.0`** — the same debounce focus changes have
  had since Phase 1. Stay quiet while moving, mention where you stopped. Turns
  90 switches into 42 landings, and the nine-switch burst into one entry.
  Flicking out and back to where you started now records nothing at all.
- **Demoted from `quiet` to `chatty`** — see the tier discussion below.

### The rate controller, and a bug that hides behind its own metric

docs/08 promises: *"the overall rate is then normalized to hit the configured
target, so tuning the factors changes which events get spoken without changing
how often it speaks."*

It did not. `k = target_rate / mean_raw`, applied once, with the quiet-period
floor and recency penalty then removing a large share of the draws *downstream*
of the normalization. A 15% target achieved **9.6%**, and because the loss was
downstream, changing any weight moved the volume too. The two dials docs/08
calls independent were coupled.

`RateController` closes the loop. It took three attempts, and the two failures
are worth recording because neither is visible from the metric being optimized.

**Attempt 1 — a single free-floating gain.** Held the achieved rate at 15–16%
across simulated days, perfectly. Meanwhile `k` random-walked between 1.4 and
8.3. Above roughly 1.0, `min(1.0, raw * k)` clamps nearly every event to
certainty, so the rate stops depending on `k` at all and is set entirely by the
quiet-period floor. The controller had no gradient left and wandered — and with
every probability pinned at 1.0, *which* event got spoken was decided by
whichever arrived first after the floor expired, not by how interesting it was.
**The rate dial was fine. The ranking had quietly stopped existing.**

**Attempt 2 — anchor the gain to the score distribution**
(`k = (target / mean_raw) × correction`), which fixed saturation. Then the
achieved rate swung between 3.7% and 35% between days. The deadband was ±10% of
target, and at this event rate (~54 eligible events a day) the standard error of
the rate estimate is about **four percentage points** — the deadband was
*narrower than the sampling noise*, so the controller read its own noise as
error and chased it forever.

**Attempt 3, kept.** Deadband sized from the statistics (two standard errors,
floored and capped), and steering at most once per quarter-window instead of on
every event. Converges to `correction ≈ 1.16` and holds:

```
last 20 simulated days:  mean 12.9%, stdev 3.6%, target 15.0%, saturation 0%
```

### What it would say now

```
verbosity      : normal  (kinds: focus, open)
needs a reason : focus (never spoken on a bare draw)
workspace      : 90 switches -> 42 landings (settle 2.0s)
target rate    : 15%   gain k=0.125
would speak    : 9/54 (16.7% achieved)
               : 1.2 utterances/hour
carried a reason: 9/9 (100%)
```

### A restart was amnesia, and amnesia is loud

Found by watching the live narrator rather than the replay, because the replay
walks one continuous history and never restarts.

The toggle is a power switch, not a mute — `prometheus off` genuinely stops
these processes (ADR-0002). So every toggle-on starts a scorer with no memory,
and for the next several events every application is "first time today" and
"rarely used" despite having been open since breakfast. With `require_reason`
on, those inflated events are **precisely the ones that get spoken**.

The symptom would have been a little burst of *"Brave, first time today"* about
applications used continuously all day, every single time the system was
switched on. Measured against this machine's journal: about six such events per
restart.

The journal is exactly the memory these processes don't have, so the scorer now
reads it at startup — counts and last-seen-day only. Deliberately *not* the
elapsed-time fields: after a restart the gap since the journal's last entry is
real, and coming back to a machine that was off for an hour genuinely is a
return.

```
focus on "the terminal" after a restart   before: novel-today, rare, odd-hour
                                          after:  odd-hour
focus on "Obsidian" (genuinely unused today)      novel-today, rare, odd-hour
```

## The verbosity ladder, re-cut

docs/01 defined the tiers by *breadth of event kind*:

```
quiet  — workspace switches only
normal — + app launches and closes
chatty — + focus changes, fullscreen, monitor changes
```

With workspace switches turning out to be the most frequent event class rather
than the rarest, that composition breaks the tier mechanically, not just
aesthetically. The rate controller speaks a fixed fraction of *eligible* events
— so when 53 of 59 eligible events at `normal` are workspace switches, ~90% of
everything the system says is a workspace number. Measured on the real journal
before the re-cut: **17 of 20 utterances** were "Two, then." / "Three now." /
"One."

Re-cut on what an utterance is worth, which is what the original ladder was
reaching for:

```
quiet  — app launches only              0.1 utterances/hour
normal — + focus changes                1.2 utterances/hour
chatty — + workspace, fullscreen, monitor  4.7 utterances/hour
```

`close` is in no tier — docs/06 already has closing a window saying nothing,
because you were there when it happened. It stays journalled, never spoken.

Both the tier composition (`attention.verbosity_kinds`) and the require-a-reason
set are config, so moving a kind between tiers is an edit, not a rebuild.

### The loudest remaining dial

`attention.require_reason` decides which kinds must carry a real signal rather
than merely winning a draw. With `focus` in that list:

| `require_reason` includes `focus` | utterances/hour | carrying a reason |
|---|---|---|
| yes *(default)* | 1.2 | 100% |
| no | 4.1 | 59% |

Defaulted to the quiet end deliberately. docs/06 opens with *"Silence is the
default. Every utterance has to earn its place against the alternative of
staying quiet"*, and docs/08 records the open risk that varied phrasing is
*harder* to tune out than fixed phrasing, not easier. Turning this up after a
quiet week is a much easier conversation than turning it down after an
irritating one.

**This is the first thing to revisit**, and it is one line in `config.json`.

## 5.1a — `min_turn_secs`, answered with numbers

Parked since Phase 3 on purpose: *"the right number is found by ear, not by
guessing."* Phase 5 went looking for the evidence and found **none had been
recorded** — `min_turn_secs` was read from config and then never used, and
nothing logged how long a turn took.

Two fixes.

**The hook now measures it.** Turn duration comes from the session transcript,
because nothing in the Stop payload carries it. Two traps, both found by reading
real transcripts rather than assuming:

- Tool results are also `type: "user"`. A turn with eleven tool calls looks like
  eleven turns unless they are excluded — which, measured against a real
  session, turned one 1325-second turn into 131 turns averaging nine seconds.
- The prompt can be a long way back. In this project's own session the last user
  prompt sat **1.78 MB from the end of the file**, so a fixed 512 KB tail window
  found nothing and reported "duration unknown" for hours. Now the window
  widens: 256 KB → 2 MB → 16 MB. Worst case measured at 83 ms, on an async hook
  that runs once per turn.

`turn_secs` is now logged on every Stop outcome whatever the threshold, so the
question answers itself from a week of real use.

**And a starting number, measured** — 154 turns across 59 of this machine's own
Claude transcripts:

```
p25 15s | p50 52s | p75 164s | p90 455s

min_turn_secs   speaks after
      30s            63% of turns
      45s            53%          <- set here
      60s            46%
```

45s sits just under the median and cuts roughly the half of turns short enough
that you were plainly still watching.

## 5.4 — Context gates

In the broker, not in each producer: ADR-0001 makes it the one place every
utterance passes through, so a gate added there covers Claude and shell
narration too, not only the ambient path that prompted it.

| gate | closed means | still speaks |
|---|---|---|
| `screen_locked` | logind `LockedHint=yes` | nothing |
| `fullscreen` | Hyprland reports fullscreen (not merely maximized) | `critical` |
| `other_audio` | a sink-input that isn't ours | `critical` |

Pulled at intent time rather than polled, which keeps docs/07 rule 1 intact —
nothing runs while the desktop is idle — with a 2-second cache so a burst of
intents shares one reading. 200 gate checks cost 1.7 ms.

Every probe fails **open**. A missing `loginctl` is not evidence that the screen
is locked, and a gate that silenced the system because a subprocess failed would
be a worse bug than the one it was added to prevent.

**One real bug, found by probing rather than reasoning.** The obvious
implementation is `loginctl show-session self -p LockedHint`. Inside a systemd
*user* unit that fails — `Caller does not belong to any known session` — and
the failure is invisible in the worst way: the gate fails open and reports "not
locked" forever, which is indistinguishable from a screen that was never locked.
Fixed by resolving the graphical session from outside it
(`loginctl show-user <uid> -p Display`), and verified with `XDG_SESSION_ID`
unset as it is under systemd.

An intent marked `user_initiated` skips all three — someone who just pressed the
read-aloud key has already answered the question these gates ask. The mic gate
is deliberately *not* skipped: it protects the dictation transcript, and
pressing a key does not make that safe.

## 5.5 — The legacy TTS scripts, routed through the broker

`omarchy-tts-read-cursor`, `omarchy-tts-terminal-live` and `terminal_feed.py`
each spawned Piper and piped it to `paplay` directly, which broke the one
acceptance criterion that is not a matter of taste: **only one thing ever holds
the audio device**. They now submit intents.

What that buys, beyond the criterion:

- **The microphone gate applies.** The old path would read four thousand
  characters over a live dictation. It now stops mid-word when voxtype starts.
- **The shut-up key works on them**, because the broker owns the utterance.
- They still work with Prometheus toggled off — each falls back to the original
  direct-Piper path, which is what docs/07's "inert but not uninstalled" means
  in practice.

Two things needed care:

**Backpressure.** `terminal_feed.py`'s old `speak()` blocked until `paplay`
finished, and that was the only thing stopping it reading stdin faster than it
could speak. Fire-and-forget would let a noisy build queue minutes of audio in
seconds and then hit the rate limiter, so the feature would appear to skip most
of its output. It uses `prometheus say --wait`, which returns when the utterance
has actually been spoken — restoring the old timing exactly.

**Toggling off.** `read-cursor` decided "am I already speaking?" from a pid file
that only ever knew about its own Piper process, and could not see an utterance
the broker was playing for it. Added `prometheus status --speaking`, which asks
the broker and prints one word.

Originals are backed up alongside each script as `*.pre-prometheus.bak`.

## 5.3 — The phrase bank: 53 lines cut, and the gaps that let them in

Six regenerations, each of which produced a *different* family of bad
phrasings once the previous family was blocked. That iteration is the finding,
not an accident of process: a validator tightened against what a model did last
time tells you nothing about where it will go next, and it goes somewhere new
every time it runs out of true things to say.

`app_odd_hour` drew **"Files changed, Claude Code."** for a window gaining
focus. The system has no idea whether any file changed.

Auditing all 152 desktop phrasings turned up 15 in the same family, every one
model-written, none hand-written:

```
"Files changed, {app}."                 <- for a window gaining focus
"{app} blinks briefly."
"Prompt waiting patiently in {app}."
"{app}'s been left running, waiting."
"The prompt remains blank in {app}."
"Claude Code's running on {project}."   <- for a BLOCKED agent
```

The failure mode is consistent and worth naming: **asked to say something about
a bare focus change, the model reaches for a detail that would make it
interesting, and every available detail is one it invented.** This is the same
thing [docs/08](../docs/08-personality-and-config.md) found when it cut the
target from forty phrasings per moment — it just survived in smaller numbers.

The `claude_blocked_project` line is worse than invented, it is backwards: it
reports a blocked agent as *running*, which is precisely the "outcomes reported
backwards" failure the acceptance criteria name.

### And two more families the regeneration then produced

Cutting those and regenerating surfaced the value of doing this as a **loop**
rather than a pass. The refilled bank contained a fresh crop, in two families
the original lists did not cover:

**Computer-science vocabulary.** The `banned_words` list was aimed at
*corporate* filler — "active", "ready", "successfully". It said nothing about:

> "New {app} process launched."     "{app} session beginning."
> "Claude's input queue empty."     "Another thread asking from Claude…"

A session and a queue are not things you hear about, they are things a program
has. docs/06 principle 3: he knows he opened an app, and being told a "process
launched" is the same fact in a worse costume.

**Personification.** A computer with a mood is the failure docs/06 names as
unacceptable at *any* register, and the model reaches for it the moment it is
short of true things to say:

> "{app}, humming quietly tonight."   "That's {app} humming along."
> "Might need coffee later for that {app} session."
> "Nothing touched in {app} still."

**And one defect that only sound would have caught.** The bank contained
*"Claudes awaits your response."* The missing apostrophe is nearly invisible on
the page and unmistakable out loud — which is the entire argument for this being
a listening review. It is now a validator rule (`plural_proper_noun`), alongside
a check that a line ends like a sentence at all, added after *"{app} session
freshly started"* was banked with no full stop and would have run straight into
the next utterance through the broker's sentence splitter.

### Where it landed

**53 phrasings cut across six regenerations** (56 vetoed in total, three of
which predate this phase), and every gap closed at the source so the family
cannot come back:

| added to | words |
|---|---|
| `unknowable_words` | file, files, changed, blinks, prompt, waiting, patiently, running, field, button, menu, … |
| `banned_words` | session, process, thread, queue, launched, initiated, we, our, us, humming, quietly, touched, … |
| new validator rules | `plural_proper_noun`, `no_terminal_punctuation` |

The distinctions matter and were made line by line, not by list:
`"Claude awaits input."` **stays** — a blocked agent genuinely awaits input, and
that is exactly what docs/06 wants that alert to say. It is the *field* in
`"Claude's input field cleared."` that is invented, not the input. Likewise
`waiting` and `prompt` remain legal for `claude_blocked` through its
`allow_words`.

Verified both directions: every cut line is now rejected by the validator, and
every keeper still passes.

**Not one hand-written seed failed any of these checks.** All the cuts were
model-written. That is the clearest evidence yet for the seeds-as-quality-floor
decision in [docs/08](../docs/08-personality-and-config.md).

### The last three came from the transcript, not the bank

Worth separating out, because they were found a different way and that is the
point. Reading what the system had *actually said* during the day's work —
`prometheus transcript`, not `phrase --show` — turned up this, spoken aloud:

> "Claude's waiting on your input— Prometheus."

A missing space before the em-dash. It reads as a typo and lands as a missing
beat. Pulling on it found two more families in the most valuable alert the
system has:

| line | defect |
|---|---|
| `"Claude's waiting on your input— {project}."` | no space before the em-dash |
| `"Answer needed for Claude - {project}."` | hyphen where the seeds use an em-dash; Piper phrases them differently |
| `"He's stuck in {project} - Claude."` | **`he` is a banned word** — and Claude is stuck, not "he" |

That last one is the interesting failure. `he` / `him` / `his` are banned
precisely to stop the system referring to Nick in the third person, and the
check was passing anyway: `normalise()` closes apostrophes up so `"didn't"`
stays a single token (deliberately — it lets an anchor like `didn` match), and
that same rule turns `"he's"` into `"hes"`, which `" he "` never matches. The
ban had a hole exactly the width of a contraction.

Three more rules, and eight more lines cut:

| rule | catches |
|---|---|
| `banned_word_contraction` | `he's`, `he'll`, `we're` — any banned word wearing an apostrophe |
| `dash_spacing` | an em-dash without a space either side |
| `hyphen_as_dash` | ` - ` where the seeds all write ` — ` |

`banned_word_contraction` matches the **original** text on a literal
apostrophe, not the normalised form. Matching the normalised form was the first
attempt and it rejected `"a well-timed question"` — normalise turns the hyphen
into a space, and `"well"` then reads as `we` + `ll`. The apostrophe is what
makes it a contraction, so the rule requires one.

**The method is the lesson.** Everything in the earlier sections was found by
auditing the bank; these three were only findable by reading what came out of
the speaker. `prometheus transcript` is not just a debugging aid for when
something goes wrong — it is where defects that survive every static check
become visible.

### And two more from drawing phrasings the way the runtime does

`prometheus phrase <moment> --slot app=Brave` draws a line exactly as the
system would. Doing that once per moment after the fourth regeneration found
two families nothing else had:

> **"Lucent is aware Brave is opening."**

The system narrating *itself*, by name, in the third person.
[docs/01](../docs/01-architecture.md) states it flatly — *"Never narrate
Prometheus's own activity"* — and this is the worse version, because it also
announces what it is about to say before saying it (docs/06 principle 4). The
authoring prompt tells the model the system's name so it can speak *as* itself;
this is that going wrong.

The rule for it checks the **configured identity**, not a fixed word, so it
survives `prometheus name "Athena"`. `claude_blocked_project` is exempt —
`{project}` legitimately renders as a project name, which on this machine is
frequently "Prometheus".

> **"Long night ahead in Brave."** · **"Complex task likely for Brave now."**

Guessing about the future, which it can see no better than the past it also
cannot see.

**And this one over-corrected first, which is worth recording.** The initial
prediction word list also banned `often`, `usually`, `typically` and `tonight`,
and immediately rejected three lines that were entirely correct:

```
"{app}. That doesn't happen often."   <- app_rare, and TRUE — it is the moment's whole job
"Unusual for {app} tonight."          <- app_odd_hour, and TRUE
```

Those words describe the present or the past. Only words that claim to know
what happens *next* belong in that list, and narrowing it to `ahead`, `likely`,
`expect`, `gonna`, `upcoming`, `tomorrow` keeps both the cuts and the keepers.

`battery_low` separately allows `soon`: a warning whose entire content is *"you
will need a cable shortly"* is the one moment where saying what happens next is
the point.

**The general shape of every one of these:** the useful rule is never "ban the
word", it is "ban the word *where it cannot be true*". Most of the new rules
needed a per-moment exemption before they were right.

### A fifth family, and the decision to stop

Running the audit once more after the fifth regeneration found, predictably,
another new family:

> "Brave's interface shows itself."  ·  "The timer's still counting for Brave."

On-screen furniture again, in words the previous round's list didn't contain.
And one that is worse than untrue — a **polarity error in the most valuable
alert in the system**:

> "Claude processing input on Prometheus."

Claude is *blocked*. "Processing" says it is working. That is the same
"outcomes reported backwards" failure as `"Claude Code's running on
{project}."` earlier, surviving in a different verb. Both `claude_blocked`
moments now carry a `banned_extra` list — `processing`, `working`, `thinking`,
`computing`, `busy`, `continuing`, `proceeding` — because a stopped agent is
the one thing those words can never describe.

("progress" went into the unknowable list with them and came straight back out:
it rejected *"Couldn't make progress on the build."*, which is idiomatic
English. The UI sense is "progress bar", and `bar` was already covered.)

**Then I stopped, deliberately, with the bank clean rather than converged.**
Six rounds produced six distinct families, and there is no reason to think a
sixth would not produce a sixth. What actually bounds the problem is not a
longer word list:

- the **seeds** are the quality floor, and not one of them failed any check in
  any round — 134 hand-written lines that are correct by construction;
- **vetoes are permanent**, so nothing cut ever returns;
- the **audit routine** is written down in [docs/06](../docs/06-voice-and-tone.md)
  to be run after every regeneration, because that is the actual control;
- and the **listening pass is Nick's**, which is the point of the phase.

## 5.6 — Re-measuring overhead, and a harness that was lying

The contract held. The harness said it didn't, and finding out which was true
took more work than the measurement.

**First run: FAIL.** 0.29 % of one core over the 600-second idle window, against
a contract of "effectively zero". Two components:

| process | CPU over the window | ctx switches |
|---|---|---|
| `piper` | **1.620 s** | **3** |
| `prometheus-hypr` | 0.110 s | 402 |
| `prometheusd` | 0.010 s | 18 |

Piper's line is self-contradictory on its face — 1.62 seconds of CPU across
*three* context switches means one long uninterrupted burst of compute, not a
process waking up repeatedly. That is not what polling looks like.

Measured directly over a genuinely quiet 90 seconds instead:

```
piper             dCPU=0.000s  dSwitches=0
prometheus-hypr   dCPU=0.000s  dSwitches=0
prometheusd       dCPU=0.000s  dSwitches=0
```

So the system was fine and the harness was wrong. It took **three** rounds to
find all of it, and each round found a genuinely different hole — worth
recording in order, because the pattern is the interesting part: every fix
narrowed what "idle" meant, and each time something else turned out to be
producing work that wasn't being counted as an event.

**Round 1 — two problems.**

1. **Piper's first synthesis after a spawn initialises the ONNX session
   lazily, across several threads**, so it bills far more CPU-seconds than it
   takes wall-clock. The script allowed a fixed `sleep 3` for the "Listening."
   toggle-on confirmation; part of that work landed after the baseline sample
   and was counted as idle overhead. One word, 1.62 s of CPU.
2. **The window was not idle.** The journal recorded two focus changes, two
   workspace switches and a fullscreen toggle during it. `prometheus-hypr`
   waking 402 times to receive real events is precisely the behaviour under
   test, not a violation of it. Phase 1 hit this exact thing and diagnosed it
   by hand (357 context switches from this session's animating title); Phase 5
   hit it again, which is the argument for fixing it in the harness rather than
   writing another note about it.

Fixed by waiting on `prometheus status --speaking` instead of guessing with
`sleep`, and by counting journal entries across the window so a non-idle one
reports **INCONCLUSIVE with the event count** rather than FAIL.

**Round 2 — the wait itself was wrong, twice.** It broke on *any* non-zero
exit, so a daemon that had not finished starting answered `unknown` and the
loop exited immediately. And "idle" is not a finish line on its own:
`prometheus on` returns before the confirmation has even been *queued*, so one
idle reading can simply mean "not started yet". Fixed by requiring idle to hold
continuously for four seconds, treating `unknown` as not-ready.

**Round 3 — the one that mattered.** Still FAIL: 1.42 s of Piper CPU across
**four context switches**, in a window the harness now certified as having zero
desktop events. Four switches is not polling and not a busy loop; it is one
long burst. Tracing a freshly spawned Piper showed its startup cost settles at
0.75 s within a second and then stays flat, so startup wasn't it either.

`prometheus transcript` had the answer:

```
05:03:47  spoken  claude  1962ms  'Another ask from Prometheus for Claude.'
```

**A Claude Code turn ending fires the Stop hook, which speaks.** That is not
desktop activity, so nothing lands in the journal and the window looks idle —
while a two-second utterance costs Piper about 1.4 s of CPU across its ONNX
threads. The harness was measuring the system doing exactly what it is supposed
to do and calling it overhead.

So the idleness check now counts **utterances as well as desktop events**, and
the INCONCLUSIVE message names both traps by name, because both are easy to
walk into while working on the machine being measured.

**The pattern across all three rounds is the lesson.** Every fix narrowed what
"idle" meant, and each time something else turned out to be producing real work
that wasn't being counted as an event. The contract is **"no CPU without an
event"** — so the hard part of testing it is not measuring CPU, it is
enumerating what counts as an event. The system measured 0.000 s every single
time it was measured cleanly.

**Round 4 — PASS**, on the full 600-second contract window:

```
RAM     142.8 MB          (budget 150 MB, single voice)
CPU     0.000 s           0.0000% of one core
ctx     2 switches        0.003 per second
desktop events during window : 0
utterances during window     : 0
VRAM    unchanged between COLD and WARM
```

Two context switches in ten minutes, across three processes. That number is the
actual proof of the no-polling rule — not the CPU figure, which a sufficiently
efficient poll loop could also fake.

Getting a clean window required not ending a Claude Code turn for the duration,
which is its own small joke about the third bug.

**Where the memory went.** `prometheus-hypr` grew **15.3 MB → 25.1 MB** when
narration was switched on: the phrase bank held resident plus the attention
scorer. That is what buys the central promise — picking a phrasing stays a
dictionary lookup with no model loaded and no VRAM held. `prometheusd` grew
0.3 MB for the context gates.

**And one number the harness cannot see.** It samples a freshly spawned Piper,
so 142.8 MB is the *floor*. Piper's RSS grows with use — 100.1 MB new, 107.8 MB
after a day of real utterances — and the same system measured **151.1 MB live**
at the end of this phase. That is **1.1 MB over the single-voice budget**.

Recorded rather than rounded away. It is not a problem (151 MB is 0.5 % of this
machine's RAM) but the honest statement is "at budget", not "within it". The
budget was set in Phase 0 against 117 MB, and ambient narration has spent most
of the slack. The second voice is currently **off**; turning it on would put
the total near **245 MB against the 250 MB two-voice budget**. The next phase
that adds a resident process should reconsider the warm-Piper flag first —
~100 MB for 0.3 s per utterance is the only line here big enough to matter.

## What is still open

- **The require-a-reason default.** 1.2/hour is a guess about taste dressed in
  evidence about frequency. One line to change; first thing to revisit.
- **`min_turn_secs: 45`** is measured against turn *lengths*, not against when
  Nick actually looks away. `turn_secs` is now logged; a week will settle it.
- **A day is not a week.** Every number here comes from one working day, and
  it is a day spent building this system, which is not a representative day.
  `replay-scoring.py --compare` re-runs the entire argument against a longer
  journal whenever there is one.

- **Task 5.7, the operator's manual, is not started.** It is the one item in
  the phase that is not evidence-driven, and it would mostly duplicate
  [docs/10](../docs/10-feedback-and-tuning.md), which already covers the
  tuning path. Worth writing once the dials above have actually settled —
  documenting a number that is about to change is wasted effort.

- **The listening review itself.** The bank is audited, the validator gaps are
  closed and the audio is rendered, but 5.3 is not finished until someone has
  heard it. That is deliberate: the whole point of docs/06's review gate is
  that *"text on a page is not evidence about speech"*, and nothing in this
  document is evidence about speech either.

  ```bash
  cat experiments/phrasebook/INDEX.md      # read first — `*` seed, `~` model
  experiments/phrasebook/play.sh           # ⚠ MAKES SOUND (~3 min)
  experiments/phrasebook/play.sh session   # the 49-second one that decides it
  ```

  The question to hold is not "is this a good sentence" but **"would I still be
  happy hearing this the fortieth time?"** Cut what fails with
  `prometheus-phrasebank veto <moment> "<the words>"`.
